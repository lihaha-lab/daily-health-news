# Everyday health briefing contract

This document is the editorial contract for the public-facing health edition.
It is versioned with the topic pack so collection, summarization, review, and
publishing can be tested independently.

## Reader promise

Help ordinary readers in mainland China follow timely heart and cerebrovascular
health news. Connect each story to everyday risk management, warning signs, or
timely help-seeking when the source supports that connection. The briefing does
not diagnose, prescribe, or replace professional medical advice.

Prioritize Chinese and local reporting. Overseas sources may be included when
they have clear relevance, but identify the country or region where a study,
recommendation, or policy applies.
International guidance must not be presented as automatically applicable in
mainland China.

## Briefing structure

Keep one Feishu document across months, with the newest day first. Give each day a
date heading and normally about three items (two to five when available). Each
item has only a bold title, source, and a one- or two-sentence summary. Put
original links, publication dates, and other traceability details in the news
table rather than the reader-facing document.

Summaries should explain what happened and, where supported, encourage routine
management of blood pressure, lipids, blood sugar, activity, and warning signs.
Do not imply that a particular behavior caused someone's illness or that a
supplement prevents or treats it. Publish fewer items when news is scarce.

## Everyday relevance gate

An item should be included only when it is recent, verifiable, and answers at
least one of these questions:

- Could it change an ordinary person's everyday health choices or understanding?
- Does it affect food, exercise, sleep, mental wellbeing, family care, or common
  consumer health products?
- Is there a clear, near-term prevention or safety implication for the audience?

Normally exclude clinician-only guidelines, drug approvals, hospital operations,
medical workforce news, technical consultations, early-stage basic research,
and international policy with no clear local or consumer impact. A regulator
notice may qualify only when ordinary consumers are directly affected; identify
the product, location, affected group, and action exactly as the source states.

## Evidence rules

- `primary/direct`: may support a briefing item on its own when the claim is
  within the issuing institution's remit.
- `authoritative/direct`: may support factual reporting, but material medical
  claims should link to the underlying study or regulator where available.
- `reputable/corroborate`: requires either a primary source or a second
  independent reputable source before publication.
- `discovery/discovery_only`: may create a review candidate but must never be
  the only published link or evidence.

## Editorial checks before publishing

- What happened.
- A faithful Chinese title when the source title is in another language, with the
  original title retained for verification.
- Why it matters.
- Possible impact on ordinary people.
- Confirmed facts versus uncertainty or interpretation.
- Applicable country or region; if the source does not say, mark it as not
  specified rather than infer it.
- For international items, whether and how the information applies to mainland
  China; say "not established" when evidence does not support a conclusion.
- Source institution and direct link, retained in the news table.
- For reported sudden cardiac deaths or collapse of a named public figure, only
  state a cause if it is explicitly confirmed by an attributable official or
  family statement. Do not infer a diagnosis from age, occupation, symptoms, or
  social-media posts; avoid sensational headlines and keep the public-health
  context separate from the individual report.

Summaries should fit one short paragraph, avoid repeating the headline or
takeaways, explain unfamiliar terms simply, and keep personal relevance separate
from confirmed facts. Never turn a study into a personal action instruction.

## Prohibited patterns

- Medical diagnosis, individualized treatment advice, or alarmist wellness copy.
- Guaranteed outcomes, cure claims, or exaggerated risk language.
- Presenting an early study, press release, or model prediction as consensus.
- Hiding geographic limits on recalls, approvals, or public-health guidance.
- Publishing an aggregator redirect when a direct primary-source URL exists.
- Changing medicine, chemical, product, dose, or unit names while translating.
- Advising readers to start, stop, or change treatment unless the source
  explicitly gives that advice to the affected group.
- Including an item merely because it comes from an authoritative institution;
  source credibility does not replace the everyday-relevance test.
